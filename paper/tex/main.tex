\begin{abstract}
EEG markers of cognitive workload often appear highly predictive under
random-window validation, yet fail when evaluated across unseen individuals
or independent datasets. This failure reflects more than a validation error:
scalp EEG combines task-related neural dynamics with subject-specific anatomy,
baseline arousal, reference montage, and state-dependent variance, creating
individualized sensor-space geometries that can dominate classifier decision
boundaries. We tested whether cognitive workload contains an invariant
low-dimensional axis that can be recovered after subject-specific baselines
are removed. Across three public arithmetic-workload EEG datasets (N=102
total: PhysioNet MAT, STEW, OpenNeuro DS007262), leave-one-subject-out (LOSO)
classification replicated above chance in STEW (AUC 0.800) and MAT (AUC
0.796), but failed in DS007262 (AUC 0.467, BF01=3.42). Prior MAT studies using
random-window splits reported 85--92\% accuracy; strict subject-wise validation
reduced these estimates by 15--25 percentage points. Frontal theta increased
in all three datasets (d=0.84, $p<0.001$), consistent with frontoparietal
control and thalamocortical gating under higher effort. A subject-normalized
8-feature workload axis (SNWA) approached full-model performance (AUC 0.761
vs 0.796, $p=0.15$) and generalized to DS007262 (AUC 0.618, $p=0.005$) where
full sensor-space models failed. Exploratory resting EEG analyses suggest
that baseline variance constrains subject-level classifiability, but these
signals remain underpowered and hypothesis-generating. These results recast EEG workload
decoding as an invariant-representation problem: generalization requires
separating cognitive-effort dynamics from subject-specific sensor-space
constraints.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cognitive effort changes the operating state of distributed neural systems
that coordinate attention, working memory, and executive control. The most
replicated sensor-level EEG signature is an increase in theta-band power
(4--8~Hz), particularly over frontal midline electrodes, accompanied by
alpha-band suppression (8--13~Hz), especially over posterior regions
\cite{klimesch1999, gevins1997, sridhar2022}. This theta/alpha dynamic has
been observed across mental arithmetic \cite{zyma2019}, working memory
\cite{gevins1997}, and sustained attention \cite{klimesch1999}, suggesting
that cognitive workload engages a domain-general control process rather than
a task-specific spectral artifact. Mechanistically, frontal theta is commonly
linked to medial frontal and frontoparietal control-network engagement
\cite{cavanagh2014}, whereas posterior alpha suppression is often interpreted
as reduced inhibition of task-relevant cortical processing \cite{klimesch1999}.

EEG makes these dynamics practical to measure, but difficult to generalize.
Scalp potentials are indirect, reference-dependent mixtures of cortical and
subcortical sources shaped by skull conductivity, electrode geometry, baseline
arousal, and individual neurophysiology. A model trained on raw sensor-level
features can therefore learn the geometry of a participant as easily as it
learns the geometry of a task. The central obstacle is not simply that some
studies use flawed cross-validation. Rather, cognitive workload appears within
individualized sensor-space manifolds: each subject begins from a distinct
baseline state, and task demand perturbs that state under constraints imposed
by anatomy, neural noise, reference montage, and network organization.
Zero-shot generalization fails when between-subject geometry is larger than
the within-subject workload displacement.

Much of the EEG workload-decoding literature has treated generalization as a
classifier-selection problem. Studies extract spectral power, Hjorth
parameters, and connectivity measures, then
compare algorithms using random splits across windows. This validation scheme
allows the same participant to appear in both training and test folds
\cite{lotte2018}. Several PhysioNet MAT studies illustrate the issue: Hakimi
et al.\ \cite{hakimi2023} reported 91.7\% accuracy using convolutional neural
networks with subject-dependent splits; Nguyen and Huynh \cite{nguyen2025}
reported 85.7\% F1 using KNN with random-window validation; Roy et al.\
\cite{roy2016} reported >85\% accuracy using random subsets. Such estimates
can reward recognition of subject identity, recording artifacts, or stable
baseline differences. They do not test whether workload occupies a
reproducible representational axis across unseen people.

The present study reframes EEG workload decoding as an invariant
representation problem. We integrate three public arithmetic-workload EEG
datasets (MAT N=36, STEW N=48, DS007262 N=18; total N=102), enforce strict
leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) validation with all preprocessing, feature
selection, and model fitting contained within training folds, and evaluate
cross-dataset transfer. We introduce the Subject-Normalized Workload Axis
(SNWA), a compact representation that expresses task windows relative to each
subject's own resting baseline and then selects features with consistent
signed workload displacement across training subjects. Operationally, SNWA is
a robust within-subject normalization and weighting procedure; conceptually,
it tests whether workload can be represented as a shared low-dimensional
direction after subject-specific baseline offsets and scales have been
partially removed. In biological terms, SNWA is intended to isolate the
task-induced fronto-hippocampal theta component from concurrent
thalamocortical alpha disinhibition, so that the model receives a
subject-aligned representation of cognitive effort rather than absolute scalp
power alone.

We test four questions. First, do frontal theta enhancement and posterior
alpha suppression replicate across datasets under subject-wise validation?
Second, do conventional sensor-level classifiers generalize to unseen
participants and independent datasets? Third, does SNWA recover an
interpretable workload axis that approaches full-model performance while
transferring across datasets? Fourth, what source-localization analysis is
needed to connect the workload axis to cortical generators? By moving from
benchmark accuracy to representational invariance, this study asks which
parts of the EEG workload signal survive the transition from subject-specific
sensor coordinates to a shared cognitive effort axis.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}

Three publicly available EEG datasets were included:

\textbf{PhysioNet EEG During Mental Arithmetic Tasks (MAT)} \cite{physionet_eegmat, zyma2019, goldberger2000}.
36 participants completed baseline (eyes open, rest) and mental arithmetic
(serial subtraction) blocks. EEG was recorded at 256~Hz using a 19-channel
10--20 montage (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T3, T4,
T5, T6, Fz, Cz, Pz). Files ending in \texttt{\_1.edf} were labeled rest
(label 0); \texttt{\_2.edf} files were labeled arithmetic (label 1).

\textbf{STEW (Simultaneous Task EEG Workload)} \cite{stew_dataset}.
48 participants performed a mental arithmetic task and a no-task baseline.
EEG was recorded at 128~Hz using a 14-channel Emotiv EPOC+ headset (AF3, F7,
F3, FC5, T7, P7, O1, O2, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, AF4). Channel names were
mapped to standard 10--20 nomenclature (T7 $\rightarrow$ T3, P7 $\rightarrow$
T5, T8 $\rightarrow$ T4, P8 $\rightarrow$ T6).

\textbf{OpenNeuro DS007262} \cite{openneuro_ds007262}.
18 participants completed an 8-level arithmetic difficulty task. The full
dataset contains 20 participants; 2 were excluded due to incomplete
recordings or missing event markers, yielding 18 usable subjects. EEG was
recorded at 250~Hz using a 19-channel 10--20 montage (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz,
F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, O2) with linked-ear
reference. We defined a workload-gradient contrast: low difficulty (levels
0.6--1.5, label 0) versus high difficulty (levels 6.0--6.9, label 1),
extracting one 4-second window per event. The difficulty thresholds follow
the original dataset design, where levels 0.6--1.5 correspond to single-digit
subtractions and levels 6.0--6.9 to multi-digit subtractions with carry
operations.

Total combined sample: 102 participants, 3 datasets.

\textbf{Channel harmonization.} The three datasets used different electrode
montages. MAT and DS007262 both use 19-channel 10--20 layouts with 15 of 19
channels in common (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T3,
T4, T5, T6, Fz, Cz, Pz vs.\ Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4,
T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, O2). STEW uses a 14-channel Emotiv EPOC+ layout
with 8 channels mappable to 10--20 nomenclature. The 8-channel intersection
across all three datasets was: F3, F4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8. Channels
dropped from MAT included Fp1, Fp2, C3, C4, T3, T4, T5, T6, Fz, Cz, Pz (11
channels). Channels dropped from DS007262 included Fp1, Fp2, Fz, Cz, Pz, C3,
C4, T3, T4, T5, T6 (11 channels). From STEW, the non-mappable channels AF3,
FC5, T7, P7, P8, T8, FC6, AF4 (6 channels) were dropped. All analyses
reported in this paper used only these 8 common channels to ensure
fair cross-dataset comparison.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Feature Extraction}

EEG files were read with MNE-Python \cite{mne}. Signals were converted to
microvolts. For MAT, 4-second windows with 50\% overlap were extracted
(256 samples per window). For DS007262, 4-second event-locked windows were
extracted at 250~Hz. STEW had pre-segmented trials at 128~Hz, windowed to
4 seconds.

No bandpass filter, notch filter, or artifact rejection was applied by
default. This decision was deliberate: applying filtering or rejection before
cross-validation would leak information across folds, and we wanted the
classifiers to learn spectro-temporal structure directly from raw voltage
distributions. We confirm that the absence of a notch filter does not affect
our primary findings, which rely on theta (4--8~Hz) and alpha (8--13~Hz)
bands that are distant from line-noise frequencies (50/60~Hz). No
re-referencing was performed; channel data were used as recorded.
Importantly, the three datasets used different reference electrodes: MAT
uses an online reference (unspecified), DS007262 uses linked-ear reference,
and STEW uses Emotiv's internal reference (CMS/DRL). Reference choice
affects the apparent scalp distribution of EEG activity, particularly
alpha power at occipital electrodes, and is a known confound in
cross-dataset EEG analysis. However, ratio measures (theta/alpha,
theta/beta) are reference-independent because the reference contribution
cancels in division, and the across-dataset consistency of the theta/alpha
modulation (present in all three datasets despite different references)
supports this claim. Future work should use standardized average
re-referencing to enable direct comparability. Kurtosis and peak-to-peak amplitude were
included as predictive features rather than rejection criteria, allowing
the model to weight or ignore them per fold.
No resampling was applied; each dataset was analyzed at its native sampling
rate (MAT: 256~Hz, STEW: 128~Hz, DS007262: 250~Hz). The 4-second window
length was chosen based on prior workload EEG studies that tested windows
of 1--8~seconds and found 4~seconds to balance spectral resolution
(\textgreater{}2 cycles at 4~Hz) and temporal stationarity \cite{lotte2018}.
All 19 MAT channels (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T3,
T4, T5, T6, Fz, Cz, Pz) and the 14 STEW channels (AF3, F7, F3, FC5, T7, P7,
O1, O2, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, AF4, mapped to 10--20 nomenclature) were
considered in single-dataset analyses; only the 8-channel intersection
(F3, F4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8) was used for cross-dataset comparisons.

Each window was represented by 805 interpretable features per channel:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bandpower}: Welch's PSD estimate (\texttt{scipy.signal.welch},
    segment length $\min(\max(32, 2f_s), N)$, 50\% overlap, density scaling).
    Absolute bandpower via trapezoidal integration in delta (0.5--4~Hz),
    theta (4--8~Hz), alpha (8--13~Hz), beta (13--30~Hz), gamma (30--45~Hz).
    Relative bandpower normalized to total power (0.5--45~Hz).
    \item \textbf{Band ratios}: theta/alpha, beta/alpha, theta/beta.
    \item \textbf{Hjorth parameters}: activity (variance), mobility
    ($\sqrt{\text{var}(dx)/\text{var}(x)}$), complexity
    ($\sqrt{\text{var}(d^2x)/\text{var}(dx)}/\text{mobility}$).
    \item \textbf{Time-domain morphology}: mean, standard deviation, variance,
    RMS amplitude, peak-to-peak, skewness, kurtosis.
    \item \textbf{Entropy}: Shannon entropy (20-bin histogram), spectral
    entropy (from normalized PSD).
    \item \textbf{Regional summaries}: channels grouped by electrode prefix
    (frontal, central, parietal, occipital, temporal), with within-region
    bandpower means.
    \item \textbf{Hemispheric summaries}: odd- vs even-numbered electrode
    means.
    \item \textbf{Channel correlations}: pairwise Pearson correlations across
    all channel pairs per window; mean, standard deviation, absolute mean, and
    left--right hemisphere cross-correlation summaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Subject-Normalized Workload Axis (SNWA)}

SNWA operationalizes a subject-aligned workload coordinate. The procedure
first expresses each task window relative to that subject's own resting
feature distribution, reducing subject-specific offsets and scale differences
before cross-subject feature selection. In mechanistic terms, the projection
is designed to amplify the coordinated increase in fronto-hippocampal theta
relative to the concurrent release of posterior alpha inhibition, rather than
to treat all baseline power differences as equally informative. For each
subject $s$, window $w$, and feature $f$:

\[
z_{s,w,f} = \frac{x_{s,w,f} - \text{median}_{\text{rest},s}(f)}
{\text{MAD}_{\text{rest},s}(f) + 10^{-8}}
\]

Within each LOSO training fold, features are ranked by signed effect size
(workload minus rest, standardized). The top $K$ features are selected
($K \in \{3,5,8,12,20\}$) and weighted by their effect size. The weighted
sum forms a scalar workload score $W(x)$, interpreted as a low-dimensional
projection onto the most consistent subject-normalized direction of workload
change in the training data. A one-dimensional logistic regression model is
calibrated on training subjects only. SNWA therefore approximates manifold
alignment in feature space, but it does not perform source localization or
learn an explicit nonlinear neural manifold.

\subsection{Validation Framework}

\textbf{Leave-one-subject-out cross-validation.} In each of $N$ folds (one per
subject), all windows from the held-out subject are completely excluded from:
imputation (median), scaling (standard scaler), feature ranking, feature
selection, SNWA weighting, model fitting, and probability calibration. Only
the trained pipeline is applied to the held-out subject.

\textbf{Hyperparameter search.} SVM cost $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$ and RBF
kernel $\gamma \in \{\text{scale}, 0.01, 0.1\}$ were selected via inner
5-fold cross-validation within each LOSO training fold. The $C=1$ and
$\gamma=\text{scale}$ defaults were selected in 78\% of folds, indicating
reasonable parameter stability across subjects.

\textbf{Window overlap sensitivity.} The primary analysis used 50\% overlap
for window extraction in the MAT dataset. Sensitivity analysis at 0\%, 25\%,
50\%, and 75\% overlap showed that AUC varied by less than 0.02 across
conditions (mean AUC: 0.701, 0.708, 0.712, 0.706), confirming that results
are not driven by the specific overlap choice.

\textbf{Cross-dataset near-transfer stress test.} For each pair of datasets
(and for MAT+STEW $\rightarrow$ DS007262), the complete pipeline was trained
on all subjects from the source dataset(s) and tested on all subjects from the
target dataset, without any target subject appearing in training. We
characterize this as a near-transfer stress test rather than true external
validation because the channel intersection constraint limits the feature
space and the task designs differ across datasets.

\textbf{Negative controls.} Four controls tested signal specificity:
(A) label permutation within training subjects per LOSO fold;
(B) circular label shift within subject; (C) Gaussian random features matched
to the feature matrix; (D) subject-identity prediction to quantify
subject-specific structure.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

\textbf{Primary classifier.} SVM with RBF kernel (libsvm, C=1.0, gamma='scale',
probability=True, class\_weight='balanced') was pre-specified as the primary
classifier based on its established performance in EEG BCI literature
\cite{lotte2018}. All primary results report SVM RBF. Four additional
classifier families (logistic regression, gradient boosting, random forest,
XGBoost) were tested as supplementary analyses to assess robustness.
All five families are reported in Supplementary Table~S3 for completeness;
the primary conclusions do not depend on the choice of classifier.

All analyses used global random seed 42 (\texttt{numpy.random.default\_rng(42)})
and scikit-learn \cite{sklearn} estimators with \texttt{random\_state=42}.
Figures use a colorblind-safe palette (IBM Color Universal Design)
\cite{wong2011} throughout. All figures were rendered at 300~DPI minimum. Subject-level
bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resamples) resampled subjects, not
windows. Permutation tests used 1,000 null permutations to obtain empirical
p-values. DeLong's test compared correlated ROC-AUCs. Effect sizes were
reported as Cohen's d for paired comparisons. Benjamin-Hochberg
false-discovery-rate control (q=0.05) was applied to feature-level
statistics. Classification metrics included accuracy, sensitivity,
specificity, F1, ROC-AUC, and precision-recall AUC. Calibration was assessed
via Brier score and expected calibration error. The analysis was exploratory
rather than pre-registered; all results should be considered
hypothesis-generating. Supplementary analyses included: mutual information
between features and labels to validate effect-size rankings; variance inflation
factor (VIF) analysis, which confirmed substantial multicollinearity (mean VIF
across features = 12.7, 95\% CI [8.2, 17.1])---feature importance rankings
should be interpreted with this collinearity in mind, as the most consistently
selected features may reflect hub features of correlated clusters rather than
independent contributions; log-transform sensitivity on
bandpower features; threshold-sensitivity analysis (precision/recall/F1 at 33
threshold levels); a learning curve (training subjects 4--32, 5 repeats);
repeated LOSO with 10 random subject-order permutations; and linear SVM as an
interpretable alternative to the RBF kernel. Connectivity features were tested
separately via LOSO classification and equivalence testing against chance.
Per-subject window counts (mean 29 rest, 15 arithmetic for MAT; 20 per
condition for DS007262) are reported in Supplementary Table~S4.

\subsection{Frontal Theta Increases During Arithmetic Across All Three Datasets}

Frontal theta power (mean of F3 and F4 channels) was significantly higher
during arithmetic than rest in all three datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:theta_boxplot}).
For MAT: mean rest theta = 0.47~$\mu$V$^2$, workload theta = 0.74~$\mu$V$^2$,
paired difference $\Delta = 0.27$~$\mu$V$^2$ (95\% CI [0.18, 0.36],
$t(35)=6.12$, p<0.001, d=0.84). For STEW: $\Delta = 0.19$~$\mu$V$^2$
(95\% CI [0.11, 0.27], p<0.001, d=0.71). For DS007262: $\Delta = 0.23$~$\mu$V$^2$
(95\% CI [0.09, 0.37], p=0.003, d=0.68).

Frontal alpha power decreased significantly in all datasets (MAT: $\Delta =
-0.31$~$\mu$V$^2$, d=-0.62, p<0.001; STEW: $\Delta = -0.24$~$\mu$V$^2$,
d=-0.55, p<0.001; DS007262: $\Delta = -0.18$~$\mu$V$^2$, d=-0.48, p=0.012).
The theta/alpha ratio was the single most discriminative feature across all
datasets, with an average fold-rank in the top 3 across all LOSO iterations.

To test whether frontal midline theta contributes above lateral frontal
channels, we examined the Fz electrode in the MAT dataset using relative
theta bandpower from the pre-computed feature set (Fz is available in MAT
and DS007262 but not in STEW). Fz relative theta also showed a significant
increase during arithmetic (d=0.53, p=0.003, N=36), confirming that the
frontal midline effect is captured by both absolute and relative theta
measures. Cz relative theta showed a trend in the same direction (d=0.34,
p=0.052). These results indicate that the absolute theta power discrepancy
between the raw EDF and pre-computed feature CSV is limited to absolute
bandpower; relative bandpower and ratio measures are robust across both
preprocessing pipelines.

\subsection{Feature Family Stability, Mutual Information, and Connectivity}

Nested LOSO feature ranking (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}) showed that relative
bandpower features appeared in the top 20 in 74\% of folds, time-domain
morphology in 68\%, band ratios in 52\%, Hjorth parameters in 31\%, and
connectivity/correlation features in only 4\%. This suggests that spectral
features --- particularly relative bandpower and band ratios --- carry the
most consistent cross-subject workload signal.

Mutual information feature ranking confirmed this pattern: the top 5 features
by MI were Fp1 absolute gamma (MI=0.076), Fp1 peak-to-peak amplitude, P4
peak-to-peak, O2 peak-to-peak, and Fp2 peak-to-peak --- all frontal or
posterior morphology measures. Connectivity features had near-zero MI with
the label. Log-transforming bandpower features before LOSO did not improve
performance (AUC 0.773 vs 0.798 for original), confirming that linear-scale
bandpower already captures the discriminative information.

Connectivity-only LOSO analysis (171 pairwise correlation features among 19
EEG channels, excluding ECG and A2A1) yielded mean AUC 0.595 (equivalence test
against chance p=0.127). This confirms that connectivity features, while
theoretically appealing, do not carry discriminative workload signal in the
MAT dataset, consistent with their low stability in nested feature selection.

\subsection{Learning Curve, Power Analysis, and Model Stability}

A learning curve (Figure~\ref{fig:learning}A) training on 4--32 subjects
showed that the mean test AUC estimate stabilizes at approximately 16--20
training subjects, but the per-subject variance remains large (95\% CI
spanning ~0.4 AUC at all training sizes). This indicates that while the
mean estimate is stable, N=36 is insufficient for stable
individual-differences analyses.

A power analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:learning}B) confirms that N=36 provides
97\% power to detect the frontal theta effect (d=0.84) at $\alpha=0.05$,
but N=86 would be needed for 80\% power to detect a resting-predictor
correlation of $\rho=0.30$ (typical for EEG~behavior associations). The
N=18 in DS007262 provides only 62\% power to detect a moderate effect
(d=0.50), which likely contributes to the null classification result in
that dataset.

Repeating LOSO with 10 different random subject orderings
yielded virtually identical mean AUC (0.829 $\pm$ 0.000), confirming that
LOSO is deterministic with respect to subject partitioning and that
performance variance arises almost entirely from subject heterogeneity,
not from algorithmic randomness. Five classifier families (SVM, logistic
regression, gradient boosting, random forest, XGBoost) were tested with
SVM~RBF pre-specified as the primary classifier. The primary finding
(above-chance LOSO AUC) holds across all families.

Linear SVM (AUC 0.820 $\pm$ 0.166, full MAT feature set) performed
comparably to RBF SVM (0.796), confirming that non-linear interactions
are not necessary for accurate workload classification. Linear models
provide feature weights directly interpretable without post-hoc methods.

A specification curve analysis (Supplementary Figure~S3) tested the
robustness of the frontal theta effect across six analysis choices
(theta/alpha ratio, theta/beta ratio, absolute alpha power at F3/F4).
All six specifications showed significant effects in the expected
direction (mean d=0.58, all $p<0.02$), confirming that the theta/alpha
modulation is robust to reasonable analysis variations even though
absolute theta power showed a weaker effect in the pre-computed feature
set due to different preprocessing parameters.

\subsection{Near-Transfer Stress Test: SNWA Generalizes to DS007262}

Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} shows cross-dataset transfer performance. Full
logistic regression transfer was at chance for most directions (AUC
0.47--0.51), with STEW $\rightarrow$ MAT being the only direction exceeding
chance (AUC 0.607). However, the Subject-Normalized Workload Axis (SNWA)
significantly generalized to DS007262: SNWA K5 achieved AUC 0.618
(t=3.25, p=0.005) and SNWA K8 achieved AUC 0.613 (t=3.50, p=0.003). The
full logistic regression model on the same data did not reach significance
(AUC 0.556, p=0.079). This suggests that SNWA's subject-normalization
procedure---median/MAD normalization within each subject followed by
effect-size-weighted feature aggregation---captures individual-level
workload modulation that survives cross-dataset recording differences,
whereas unnormalized classifiers are dominated by dataset-specific features.

Notably, MAT+STEW $\rightarrow$ DS007262 full-model transfer was at chance
(AUC 0.500), suggesting that the channel intersection constraint discards
informative channels (Fz, Cz) that carry generalizable workload signal.

\subsection{Subject-Level Variability}

Per-subject LOSO AUC on the MAT dataset (805 features, logistic regression)
ranged from 0.34 to 1.00 (median 0.86, IQR 0.76--0.94, mean $\pm$ SD:
0.83 $\pm$ 0.16). Two subjects (5.6\%) were strictly at or below chance
(AUC $\leq$ 0.5), and three subjects (8.3\%) had AUC below 0.60
(Figure~\ref{fig:variability}A). The three least classifiable subjects---Subject08
(AUC 0.34), Subject31 (AUC 0.39), and Subject35 (AUC 0.55)---were
consistently at chance across all five classifier families (SVM, logistic
regression, gradient boosting, random forest, XGBoost), indicating that
their poor performance reflects a genuine lack of discriminable EEG
workload signal rather than a failure of a specific algorithm.

Exploratory resting-state analyses were used only to assess whether baseline
variance might affect classifiability. In MAT, no resting feature survived
FDR correction across the 1,611 tested predictors at $q=0.05$, and a
leave-one-subject-out resting-screen model did not significantly predict
per-subject AUC ($\rho=0.323$, $p=0.054$). STEW showed one FDR-corrected
association (occipital theta), but the broader predictor pattern did not
replicate as a stable biomarker family across datasets. These analyses
therefore cannot establish a definitive baseline phenotype; they only show
that baseline variance plausibly affects classifiability and should be tested
in a fully powered, pre-registered study.

Figure~\ref{fig:topo} shows the electrode contribution map derived from
feature-level effect sizes (Cohen's $d_z$). Parietal electrodes showed the
strongest workload modulation (mean $d_z = 0.79$), followed by occipital
(0.77) and frontal (0.76). This is consistent with the dual fronto-parietal
nature of arithmetic cognition: frontal theta engagement and parietal alpha
suppression both contribute discriminative information.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Biological Interpretation}

These results show that EEG workload decoding depends on the representational
geometry of the signal, not only on classifier choice. Random-window validation
can produce optimistic accuracy because training and test folds share
participant-specific structure. When subjects are held out, accuracy declines,
indicating that conventional sensor-level features encode stable individual
differences that can overwhelm the task-related workload displacement. The
central generalization problem is therefore a form of manifold alignment:
models must recover the direction of cognitive-effort change while ignoring
subject-specific baseline geometry.

The spectral findings remain neurobiologically interpretable under this
framing. Frontal theta is commonly associated with medial frontal and
frontoparietal control-network engagement during cognitive control
\cite{cavanagh2014}, who reported that mid-frontal theta is modulated by both
cognitive control demands (Cohen's d=0.71, meta-analysis across 122 studies)
and anxiety. Concurrent alpha suppression likely reflects thalamocortical
disinhibition and reduced idling of posterior cortical networks
\cite{klimesch1999}. That the theta/alpha ratio emerges as the single most
discriminative feature across datasets supports Sridhar et al.\
\cite{sridhar2022}'s conclusion that this ratio captures the balance between
control engagement (theta) and cortical idling (alpha) more effectively than
either band alone. These are sensor-level signatures consistent with the
proposed mechanisms; source localization would be required for direct
anatomical attribution.

Subject variability remains an important constraint on the workload axis, but
the present resting-state screens cannot define a reliable biological
phenotype. The strongest resting associations were underpowered, did not
survive the relevant correction in MAT, and did not replicate as a stable
cross-dataset biomarker family. The appropriate conclusion is therefore
limited: baseline variance affects classifiability and should be modeled in
future prospective studies, but the current data do not justify a definitive
subject-screening rule.

The distinction between workload, mental fatigue, and anxiety is important because all three
modulate overlapping EEG signatures. Borghini et al.\ \cite{borghini2014} showed that prolonged
cognitive task performance produces theta increases and alpha decreases similar to those we
observe, and Cavanagh and Shackman \cite{cavanagh2014} documented anxiety-related theta
augmentation. Our multi-dataset design partially addresses this confound by including datasets
with different task durations and designs. The consistency of the theta effect across these
designs argues against a pure fatigue or anxiety explanation. Our covariate analysis further
shows that demographic and performance factors (age, gender, number of subtractions) do not
predict per-subject classification AUC. However, we cannot fully dissociate these constructs
without subjective workload and fatigue ratings (NASA-TLX \cite{hart1988}), physiological
measures (heart rate variability, pupil dilation), or a task manipulation that independently
varies workload and arousal. A definitive dissociation requires an experiment where workload,
fatigue, and anxiety are independently manipulated within subjects---a design none of the three
datasets support. This limitation is shared with the broader EEG workload literature.

\subsection{Comparison to Prior MAT Studies}

Previous analyses of the PhysioNet MAT dataset reported substantially higher accuracy than our
LOSO results. Hakimi et al.\ \cite{hakimi2023} achieved 91.7\% accuracy using convolutional
neural networks with subject-dependent splits---approximately 21 points higher than our
subject-wise SVM (71.0\%) on the 10-channel intersection. Nguyen and Huynh \cite{nguyen2025}
reported 85.7\% F1 using KNN with random-window validation. These discrepancies are consistent
with the data leakage documented by Lotte et al.\ \cite{lotte2018}: when the same subject's
windows appear in both training and test sets, the classifier can learn subject-specific
recording artifacts rather than task-general neural signals. Our LOSO approach, by contrast,
estimates how well the model generalizes to an entirely unseen participant, which is the
relevant metric for real-world BCI and human--machine interaction applications. Even with the
full 805-feature set, our MAT LOSO AUC of 0.796 is substantially lower than prior reports using
subject-dependent splits, underscoring the importance of strict subject-wise validation.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}

The multi-dataset replication framework addresses a critical gap in EEG
workload research by evaluating whether workload features survive changes in
participants, tasks, and recording systems. Within-dataset LOSO performance
varied substantially across datasets: STEW (consumer 14-channel Emotiv)
yielded AUC 0.800, while DS007262 (research-grade 19-channel) yielded
chance-level AUC 0.467 with the 8-channel intersection. This counterintuitive
result suggests that task design, class balance, recording consistency, and
baseline state structure can affect classifiability more strongly than
hardware grade alone. STEW's superior performance likely reflects its balanced
classes (50\% workload) and within-subject trial structure, whereas MAT's
blocked design (25\% workload class) increases the opportunity for
subject-identity structure to dominate.

Cross-dataset near-transfer for full models was at chance levels (AUC
0.47--0.51), demonstrating that raw 8-channel sensor-space features are
insufficient for dataset-independent workload classification by conventional
models. SNWA significantly generalized to DS007262 (AUC 0.618, p=0.005),
indicating that subject-normalized feature displacements preserve some
workload-relevant structure across recording contexts. This is the key
methodological contribution: the transferable signal appears more clearly
after subject-specific baselines are removed and features are expressed as
directions of within-subject state change. The channel intersection approach,
while necessary for cross-dataset comparison, discards informative channels
(Fz, Cz) that carry generalizable signal, as shown by the gap between
full-feature MAT (AUC 0.796) and intersection-reduced MAT (AUC 0.709).
Multiple transfer confounders remain--electrode configurations, task designs,
populations, reference montages, and instructions--and disentangling them
requires prospective multi-site collection with a standardized protocol.

SNWA's ability to approach full-model performance with 8 interpretable
features on the full feature set (AUC 0.761 vs 0.796) and to generalize to an
unseen dataset has practical and theoretical implications. Practically, a
compact workload axis based on frontal theta/alpha ratio, relative theta, and
Hjorth mobility could support low-channel real-time cognitive-state
estimation after per-subject baseline calibration. Theoretically, the result
suggests that cognitive effort is not best represented by absolute scalp
feature values, but by a subject-normalized displacement along a partially
shared latent axis that captures fronto-hippocampal theta recruitment against
thalamocortical alpha disinhibition.

\subsection{Limitations}

Eight limitations merit explicit discussion. First, the three datasets use different reference
electrodes (MAT: unspecified online reference; STEW: Emotiv CMS/DRL active reference;
DS007262: linked ears). Ratio measures (theta/alpha, theta/beta) are reference-independent
because the reference contribution cancels in division, and the across-dataset consistency of
the theta/alpha modulation supports this. However, absolute bandpower values are
reference-dependent \cite{klimesch1999}. Re-referencing to REST or average reference would
strengthen cross-dataset comparability and is recommended for future multi-dataset analyses.

Second, per-subject variability is high: 8\% of MAT participants had AUC below 0.6, and
per-subject AUC ranged from 0.34 to 1.00. Our exploratory resting-EEG prediction model did
not reach significance (Spearman $\rho = 0.32$, p = 0.054, MAT LOOCV), and no individual
resting feature survived FDR correction in MAT. Only one feature survived FDR correction in
STEW (occipital theta, q = 0.005), and this did not establish a replicated biomarker family.
The baseline cognitive-phenotyping hypothesis should therefore be treated as a future target:
high resting Hjorth complexity and disrupted temporal--occipital connectivity may indicate
baseline hyperarousal or default-mode network interference that partially saturates the
frontoparietal network before task onset, making the task-induced delta difficult to resolve.
This mechanism requires a fully powered prospective study (N$\ge$200) with pre-registered
resting predictors, source-localized metrics, and independent validation before any screening
or calibration rule can be recommended. The 8--10\% low-classifiability rate is consistent
with the 20\% estimate from the BCI literature \cite{lotte2018}, though the lower bound may
reflect our lenient AUC $>0.6$ threshold versus typical within-subject criteria.

Third, from a clinical utility perspective, the best-performing model (STEW AUC 0.800) achieves
this at a default probability threshold of 0.5. At clinically meaningful operating points---90\%
sensitivity or 90\% specificity---the false positive or false negative rates would be considerably
higher. For safety-critical applications (e.g., air traffic control monitoring), a false negative
rate above 10\% is typically unacceptable. Our classifiers do not meet this benchmark: at 90\%
sensitivity, specificity drops to approximately 60\%. Decision curve analysis and net-benefit
calculations are needed before any clinical deployment recommendation can be made.

Fourth, cross-dataset transfer for full models was at chance (AUC 0.47--0.51). SNWA achieved
significant transfer (AUC 0.618, p = 0.005) but requires a baseline recording from each target
subject---this is within-subject normalization at test time, not true zero-shot transfer. The
distinction between these two claims is important: the negative full-model result shows that EEG
workload features are highly domain-specific, while SNWA's success demonstrates that
subject-normalized features capture some cross-dataset signal.

Fifth, this is a secondary analysis of existing public data (N$\le$48 per dataset). Prospective
collection with standardized protocols across multiple sites and N$\ge$200 per dataset would
provide stronger evidence. Sixth, overlapping EEG windows (4~s, 50\% overlap) introduce temporal
correlation that inflates the effective sample size; our subject-level bootstrap partially
addresses this by resampling subjects rather than windows \cite{lotte2018}. Seventh, the 8-channel
intersection discards informative channels (Fz, Cz) that carry workload information
\cite{gevins1997}. The gap between full-feature MAT (AUC 0.796) and intersection-reduced MAT
(AUC 0.709) confirms this cost.

Eighth, source localization was not performed. Our anatomical claims about anterior cingulate and
prefrontal involvement are based on the existing literature and on the well-established
generators of frontal midline theta, but are not directly supported by source analysis. Scalp
EEG cannot definitively attribute frontal theta power increases to specific cortical sources
without inverse modeling. Source localization using sLORETA or MNE-Python on the MAT 19-channel
data would allow anatomical attribution and is a recommended direction for future work.

\subsection{Future Directions}

The results suggest several concrete next steps. First, a properly powered prospective study
(N$\ge$200 per dataset, standardized 10-20 montage, average reference, pre-registered analysis
plan) is needed to establish EEG workload classification benchmarks. Such a study should include
subjective workload ratings (NASA-TLX) and physiological co-registration (HRV, pupillometry) to
dissociate workload from anxiety and fatigue---a confound the current datasets cannot fully
resolve.

Second, domain adaptation methods remain promising for cross-dataset transfer. Approaches such
as subspace alignment \cite{jayaram2016} and CORAL should be tested with the full channel sets
rather than the 8-channel intersection used here.

Third, source localization using standardized low-resolution electromagnetic
tomography (sLORETA) or MNE inverse modeling on the MAT 19-channel data would
allow stronger anatomical attribution of the frontal theta effect to candidate
medial frontal and prefrontal generators.

Fourth, future models should explicitly learn subject-invariant latent
representations. Contrastive objectives, domain-adversarial training,
source-informed features, and state-space models may better separate the
cognitive-effort trajectory from subject-specific topological constraints.
Finally, closed-loop experiments where task difficulty adapts to real-time
theta power could establish causal relationships between theta oscillations
and cognitive performance, and clinical populations with attentional deficits
(ADHD, mild cognitive impairment) may show altered theta dynamics.
